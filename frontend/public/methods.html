Methods

What the numbers on the map mean, where they come from, and what they cannot tell you.

What this map shows

Every neighborhood in the 48 contiguous states and the District of Columbia gets a score for seven crimes the FBI counts: murder, rape, robbery, aggravated assault, burglary, larceny/theft and motor vehicle theft. 100 is the national average. 200 means twice the national average, 50 means half. The score describes a place. It is not anyone's chance of being a victim.

Two measures

Crime exposure divides recorded offenses by what each crime happens to. Each offense has its own denominator, and every one of them is modeled rather than counted. Murder, rape, robbery and assault use a fixed blend of modeled resident population, modeled daytime population and workplace jobs. Burglary uses residential and weighted commercial premises. Car theft uses household and commuter vehicle exposure. Larceny adds shopping and other destinations and retail jobs to the person blend. Every denominator is then rescaled so that its national total equals U.S. resident population, which is what keeps 100 the national average. So a downtown block is measured against a modeled activity base that can be far larger than the number of people who sleep there — but that base is an estimate, not a count of visitors, and no part of this map measures footfall.

Per resident divides the same offenses by the people who live there. It answers a different question, and it gives very high scores to commercial districts, campuses and transit hubs where few people sleep. Both measures are scaled so 100 is the U.S. average.

Year and geography

Estimates are for 2025, on 2020 census boundaries. Most crimes are published for census block groups, areas of roughly 600 to 3,000 residents. Murder and rape are published for census tracts only: a single year holds too few of them to say anything about an area as small as a block group. Zoomed out, the map shows tracts, then county averages.

The three composites

Overall, Violent and Property combine the single-offense numbers, weighted by how common each offense is nationally. The two measures build them differently, and neither is simply “all seven offenses published together”. On the crime-exposure measure a block group has no murder or rape value of its own, so those two terms are taken from the block group's parent tract; if the tract publishes no value the composite is withheld. On the per-resident measure the composites are built from offense counts over a shared denominator, and murder and rape do not veto a block-group composite at all, because their block-group counts are published even where their rates are not. A missing value for any of the other five offenses withholds the composite on both measures.

Where the numbers come from

Police agencies report offense totals to the FBI. Those totals cover a whole city or county, not a neighborhood. We spread each agency's total across the neighborhoods it patrols using what is known to draw each kind of crime — residents, workers, shops, bars, schools, parking, roads — and we calibrate that against the cities that publish incident-level data. Where an agency did not report a full year, its total is estimated from its own history and from comparable agencies in the same state.

The card reports both steps, separately. The source line says whether the neighborhood number is built mainly from incident records located in that neighborhood or from modeled allocation of an agency total. The jurisdiction line says whether the agency total behind it was reported in full for the data year or estimated; it is absent where no single agency total applies, as in areas covered only by a statewide remainder.

How certain this is

Most block-group values rest on modeled distribution rather than on incidents counted in that block. Read broad patterns; treat a difference of a few points between neighboring blocks as noise. We build an uncertainty range internally, but in held-out testing it covered the truth less often than it claimed to, so this edition publishes no range and no reliability rating rather than one you cannot rely on.

Six limits

  1. Only crime reported to and recorded by police appears here. A great deal of crime is never reported.
  2. Agencies differ in how they classify offenses and how completely they report them.
  3. Neighborhood values are modeled shares of agency totals, not counts of incidents.
  4. Murder and rape exist at tract level only; there is no block-group value to show.
  5. Boundaries are 2020 census geographies, which is not how people describe neighborhoods.
  6. Parks, campuses, prisons, airports and industrial areas have unusual denominators: very few residents, or an activity base much larger than the residential one. Their scores need separate interpretation and are not comparable with a residential block. Those areas can be marked on the map; the marking is an annotation, and it does not explain any individual score.

Technical methods

The full specification — temporal kernels, hierarchical agency estimation, the three-expert allocation mixture and its weights, denominator construction, special-use classification, reconciliation to national offense anchors and the validation tables — is published separately in the project's technical documentation: methodology, field dictionary, evaluation and build, in the source repository. Field-level definitions for every published column are also in fields_block_group.csv and fields_tract.csv.
