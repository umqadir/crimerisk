The most important correction to the framing is that these are not degraded point data. They are observations on a different spatial support, a classic change-of-support problem. OUP Academic
1. A non-negotiable validation correction
Do not use a model-informed grid→BG or ZIP→BG allocation as the “truth” against which you score the same frozen BG model.
Suppose the frozen model is pbp_bpb​, and you allocate a grid total among its overlapping BGs partly in proportion to pbp_bpb​. The resulting pseudo-truth is mechanically attracted toward pbp_bpb​. A low BG-level TVD then partly measures the allocation rule’s reuse of the model, not predictive accuracy. ZIP raking has the same problem: within each ZIP, the pseudo-truth usually preserves the model’s relative BG shares by construction.
For coordinate-free targets, separate three evaluations:


Target-city native-support score. Compare observed grid, ZIP, or ZIP×service-area shares with the corresponding predictions from a crosswalk/exposure operator that was frozen before examining those counts.


BG recovery score. Estimate this only in other cities where coordinates supply independent BG truth, after synthetically degrading those observations.


Production posterior. Combine the frozen model and target-city areal evidence, but do not call its allocated BG counts “truth.”


The native-support score is still valuable: it can show that the model misses the east–west, central–peripheral, or other coarse pattern. It simply is not a BG-accuracy estimate.
There is a related information issue. San Antonio’s 153,889 rows make its roughly 80–90 ZIP totals precise, but ZIPs alone provide at most roughly 79–89 independent geographic contrasts after conditioning on the city total—not 153,889 BG observations. More than 800 contrasts among the 920 BGs remain unidentified. Phoenix’s much finer grid has a far larger geographic information budget. A good posterior updates only the contrasts visible at the reported support and leaves the unobserved contrasts near the prior.

The right hybrid: address strings as uncertain spatial supports
For [code], the data do not identify a point. They identify one or more street-segment portions corresponding to an anonymized address range. Treat that as a linear or buffered support:


Normalize the street name and find all candidate centerline segments.


Restrict candidates to the reported Phoenix grid first.


Use address-range overlap, address-point density, parcel frontage, or segment length to weight candidate portions.


Intersect the candidate support with BGs.


Assign the record exactly only when essentially all candidate mass lies in one BG; otherwise retain fractional support.


For an intersection, use the crossing node only when it is unambiguous and not on a BG boundary; otherwise distribute over the touching road/corner support.


The Census documentation itself warns against treating a TIGER interpolation as an observed location: TIGER ranges may include possible structure numbers even where structures do not exist, and returned coordinates are interpolated or approximated along the range. Census.gov
A city-maintained, versioned 2024 street centerline and address-range layer would generally be preferable to TIGER where available, with TIGER as a documented fallback.
A practical confidence hierarchy would be:


Gold: unique candidate support, consistent with grid, essentially one BG.


Silver: unique street support but overlaps multiple BGs; use fractional BG weights.


Ambiguous: multiple plausible supports; use the grid allocation.


Conflict: geocoder and grid disagree materially; retain grid, flag the record, and investigate systematically.


For the hybrid estimate, subtract the accepted address-supported records from their grid totals and allocate only the remaining records by the grid prior. Do not use the successful geocodes to infer the spatial distribution of failed geocodes unless that selection model has itself been validated.

Critical simulation corrections
Do not validate a block-address geocoder by reverse-geocoding known points to TIGER hundred blocks and then geocoding them back with TIGER. That is circular and omits real source-string errors. A3′ needs cities containing both actual published address strings and independent coordinates or parcel/address-point truth. Without such a validation set, keep address refinement experimental.
For ZIPs, assigning each known point to its Census ZCTA tests only an idealized geometry. It omits:


reported-ZIP versus ZCTA disagreement;


unique and PO-box ZIPs;


mailing versus incident-location ZIPs;


vintage changes;


nongeographic placeholders;


city-boundary and annexation effects.


Use cities that publish both coordinates and record ZIP wherever possible. Treat ideal point-in-ZCTA censoring as an upper-bound experiment, not the expected production result.
For grids, randomize lattice phase and orientation. A single lattice can produce a lucky or unlucky alignment with BG boundaries and the street network.
If the coordinate “truth” city publishes only block centroids, records near BG borders should be represented as uncertain supports rather than treated as exact points. Otherwise the validation target itself contains deterministic boundary bias.

Keep TVD as the primary metric, but it is insufficient for the failure mode you care about. TVD assigns the same loss whether mass is misplaced across one street or across the city.

5. Spatially biased failure modes
Block-address and intersection geocoding
Spatially selective match failure. New subdivisions, private streets, mobile-home parks, alleys, informal locations, freeways, parks, and renamed roads may fail disproportionately. A located-only subset then overrepresents older, regular-grid neighborhoods.
False unique directional or alias match. Missing or mishandled [code], frontage-road names, aliases, suffixes, or duplicate street names can move incidents to another part of the city rather than add random noise.
Hundred-block midpoint displacement. The implied range may span several centerline segments or BGs; collapsing all records to one midpoint systematically concentrates them.
Centerline boundary bias. BG boundaries often follow roads. A centerline point can lie exactly on the boundary, and GIS tie-breaking may send every road incident to the same side. Split or buffer boundary-street supports when the address side is unknown.
Intersection corner bias. An intersection node can touch three or four BGs. Assigning the node to one polygon creates deterministic corner favoritism.
Facility-entrance collapse. Malls, campuses, apartment complexes, hospitals, airports, and large parcels may use one street entrance while incidents occur across a multi-BG footprint.
Street-layer vintage drift. New streets and renamings create a suburban/outskirt bias rather than uniform noise.
Grid-constrained false matching. Restricting candidates to GRID is helpful, but if the reported grid is itself wrong, it can force a plausible-looking wrong address. Retain a pre-constraint conflict diagnostic.
Address-field semantic error. Verify that the string is the incident location, not reporting-party, victim, owner, station, or administrative address.
Redaction mechanism differences. Sensitive offenses or certain report channels may receive more aggressive location masking, making geocode quality offense- and neighborhood-dependent.
Grid allocation
Boundary clipping and jurisdiction leakage. A grid can straddle Phoenix and non-Phoenix territory. Do not normalize the entire cell into city BGs. Include an explicit outside-universe component and reconcile it to the feed’s actual jurisdiction semantics.
Grid-layer vintage or ID drift. A current ArcGIS layer may not represent the geometry used to generate 2024 record IDs. Monthly discontinuities in grid frequencies are a useful warning.
Wrong within-cell exposure. Area weighting pushes mass into empty land; population weighting pushes it away from retail, roads, parking, and employment districts.
Large-facility smearing. An airport, stadium, mall, jail, or campus can dominate a grid while occupying only one portion or BG.
Grid assignment not based on incident point. Dispatch systems may assign by caller address, nearest address, reporting location, or a snapped centroid.
Nonrandom missing GRID. The 0.24% missing may disproportionately involve online reports, uncertain locations, highways, or sensitive incidents.
Topological slivers and boundary convention. Small grid/BG slivers can receive implausible probability if the overlay is not thresholded and mass-conserving.
Model-induced circular confidence. Model-weighted allocation followed by full-count posterior weighting can paint the prior back onto the map with enormous apparent certainty.
ZIP and service-area allocation
Reported ZIP versus ZCTA mismatch. This includes mailing routes, unique institutions, businesses, PO boxes, and areas with no ZCTA representation.
2020-versus-2024 vintage drift. A 2020 ZCTA polygon is not an exact representation of all ZIP assignments in a 2024 record feed.
Noncontiguous ZIPs. Treating one ZIP as a compact polygon can silently join unrelated areas.
Mailing address versus event location. Campuses, complexes, corporate facilities, and centralized mailrooms can place incidents in a ZIP different from their physical point.
City-crossing ZIPs. Hard normalization can pull suburban or unincorporated share into the San Antonio BG universe, or discard legitimate city portions.
Modal-ZIP BG assignment. Assigning each BG to one ZIP moves the entirety of crossing BGs across boundaries and maximizes seams. Use intersection pieces.
Small-ZIP multiplier explosions. A small expected ZIP count with a moderate observed count produces a huge hard-raking factor.
Postal-boundary seams. Constant within-ZIP multipliers create unsupported step functions in the correction field.
Service-area semantic mismatch. The field may indicate responding unit, report desk, or organizational ownership rather than location.
Impossible ZIP×area combinations. Do not force these into the nearest overlap. Preserve them as a mismatch category; their frequency is an observation-error estimate.
Shared feed-to-level risks
The geography mechanism cannot repair a spatially nonexchangeable counting-rule mismatch.
Phoenix’s primary-offense-only texture and San Antonio’s Group-A offense rows should be checked against the definition of each official LEVEL series. Multi-offense incidents, hierarchy suppression, duplicate offense rows, and differing incident-versus-offense definitions can vary spatially—for example, around nightlife locations, commercial theft destinations, or complex violent incidents. Simulate those counting rules in coordinate cities where possible.
Also verify:


occurrence date versus report date;


city boundary and police-service jurisdiction;


records at airports, transit systems, schools, or other special jurisdictions;


duplicate and amended reports;


offense-specific location redaction;


month-to-month appearance or disappearance of support codes.
